Part A: How to Use This Guide

Everything you need to facilitate Bible Truth Foundations Part 1 with confidence

Who this series is for

Bible Truth Foundations Part 1 is designed for a mixed group: new believers who have little or no prior Bible knowledge alongside mature believers who may have been in faith for years or decades. Both are in the same room at the same time, working through the same sixteen lessons. Your task as facilitator is to lead a session that genuinely serves both groups without talking down to one or losing the other.

Before your first session, be clear in your own mind about who is likely in the room. New believers need permission to ask basic questions without embarrassment. Mature believers need to be drawn deeper into truths they think they already know. The session design serves both, but only if you actively use it that way. Neither group is a problem to manage; both are assets to the room.

Running a mixed-ability group

The greatest practical challenge in this series is not the theology; it is the range of experience in the room. Your two jobs are keeping new believers engaged without patronising them, and keeping mature believers challenged without sidelining the newcomers.

Use new believers as the room’s theological checkpoint. When a new believer asks a basic question, treat it as a gift. It usually exposes an assumption that even the mature believers have never examined. Do not rush past it or answer it quietly. Let the group engage with it.

Use mature believers as guides, not teachers. Invite experienced group members to share personal application rather than theological explanation. “What has this truth cost you to hold?” or “Where did this break down in practice for you?” keeps mature believers engaged at a deeper level without turning them into a secondary facilitator who talks over newcomers.

Never presuppose prior knowledge in public. When you ask a question, do not frame it in a way that embarrasses someone who does not know the answer. Ask “What does this word mean to you?” before “What does this Greek word mean?” The room sets its temperature in the first five minutes.

Watch for the performance dynamic. In a mixed group, some mature believers will feel pressure to demonstrate knowledge. Some new believers will feel pressure to keep up. Both dynamics close down honest conversation. Reframe explicitly: the aim of every discussion is not the correct theological answer but genuine engagement with what the scripture is actually saying.

The five callout types: a facilitator’s guide

The student lessons contain five types of callout box. You need to know how to use each one in a group setting.

.note (amber border) — A note box flags something in the lesson that requires slow reading or careful attention. In a group, these are your cue to pause. Read the note aloud and ask whether anyone has a question or reaction. Note boxes are particularly valuable for drawing in new believers because they explain things that an experienced reader might skip over.

.depth (navy border) — Depth boxes go beyond the core lesson into biblical background. In a group session you are not required to cover them; they are there for students who want to go further on their own. However, when you have mature believers in the room, pointing to a depth box and saying “there is more on this question if you want to go deeper after the session” keeps them engaged without derailing the pace for the whole group.

.tip (green border) — Practical application tips. In a group setting, read these aloud near the end of the session and ask: “Is this something you could do this week? What would it actually look like in your situation?”

.caution (red border) — Caution boxes name a common misapplication of the lesson’s teaching. These are extremely useful in group discussion because they name the mistake before someone makes it. If you notice a student drifting toward the misreading during discussion, you have the language to correct it gently without singling anyone out.

.confidence (blue border, bolded label) — the most important for you to handle well. Confidence boxes flag a genuinely contested theological position: one that Bible-believing Christians within different traditions have sincerely disagreed about for centuries. When you encounter a confidence callout in the student lesson, your job as facilitator is not to adjudicate the debate or endorse one position. It is to acknowledge that the debate exists, explain briefly what both positions hold and make clear that the lesson is presenting one position clearly while respecting that other traditions take a different view. Specific language for each confidence callout is given in the relevant lesson section of this guide.

The question-first approach

Every lesson in this series opens with a personal question before any teaching begins. This is deliberate. The opening question does two things: it surfaces where each person in the room actually is before the lesson shapes their thinking, and it establishes that this is a space where personal honesty is welcome.

As facilitator, your job with the opening question is not to summarise the range of answers or move quickly to the teaching. Take the time to let two or three people respond. If the answers cluster together, notice it aloud: “Several of us seem to be coming from a similar place on this.” If the answers differ significantly, name the variety: “We are coming at this from quite different starting points, which is exactly what makes the discussion worth having.” Then use that as your bridge to the teaching: “Let’s look at what the lesson says and see whether it changes or confirms where we started.”

The opening question is also your diagnostic tool. A new believer who gives an honest, uncertain answer to the opening question has told you where to focus your attention during the teaching. A mature believer who gives a confident doctrinal answer has told you to push them further than the surface during discussion.

Lesson 1 of 16 | Section A: The Gospel Foundation

Eternal Life

At a Glance
Lesson AimStudents understand that eternal life is not primarily a future destination but a living relationship with God that begins now, and can describe what that means for how they live today.
Key ScriptureJohn 3:16–17 (NKJV)
Core TruthEternal life is not primarily a destination after death. It is a living, personal relationship with God that begins the moment you receive Jesus Christ.
Sensitivity LevelLow — No contested positions; broad evangelical consensus. Handle gently for anyone whose understanding of faith has been primarily fear-based.
Suggested Session Time60 minutes standard.
Core Truth

Eternal life is not primarily a destination after death. It is a living, personal relationship with God that begins the moment you receive Jesus Christ.

Opening Question Facilitation

Note

Opening question: “If someone asked you what ‘eternal life’ means, what would you say? What does the phrase bring to mind?”

Expect a wide spread of answers here. New believers are likely to say “going to heaven” or “living forever after you die.” Mature believers may give more nuanced answers about relationship with God, but many will still default to the future-destination framing without realising it. Both responses set up the lesson well. Let two or three people share, then say something like: “Most of us probably started with a version of the same answer. What this lesson does is take what Jesus Himself said about eternal life in John 17:3, and show that His definition is different from the one most people carry.” That single bridging sentence positions the teaching as a discovery rather than a correction.

Core Teaching Walkthrough

More than avoiding punishment. This subsection uses John 3:16–17 to establish that God’s purpose in sending Jesus was not judgement but rescue. The lesson flags the caution box’s point directly: reducing the gospel to “avoiding hell” turns it into a future benefit with no present relevance. Emphasise this for new believers who may have been told to “get saved” without being told what they were saved into. Emphasise it differently for mature believers who intellectually know better but may still relate to God primarily through a sense of what they must avoid rather than what they have received.

What eternal life actually is. John 17:3 is the pivot of this lesson. The lesson notes that the Greek word translated “know” carries the weight of the most intimate possible relationship. Do not rush past this. Read the note box aloud: it points to Genesis 4:1 and the comparison to the closest possible human union. This is the moment new believers often experience a genuine reorientation. For mature believers, ask: “If this is what eternal life actually is, how does that change the way you spend time with God?”

Eternal life begins now. 1 John 5:11–12 uses the present tense: “he who has the Son has life.” The lesson is making the point that eternal life is not something you are waiting for; it is something you currently possess. This is an important pastoral reframe for anyone whose Christianity has felt entirely future-oriented or conditional. The depth box connects this to 1 Corinthians 6:17 and union with Christ. Flag the depth box for mature believers.

Life in abundance, starting today. John 10:10 brings in Galatians 1:4 — “to deliver us from this present evil age.” The lesson is making the point that the gospel addresses the present, not only the future. This subsection is particularly important for anyone in the group who is struggling currently. Abundance is not promised as prosperity but as life with God in the present tense. The tip box is worth reading aloud here: it reframes prayer and Bible reading as time with someone who wants to know you, not duty or transaction.

Discussion Guide

Read John 3:16. What does this verse say God’s purpose was in sending Jesus into the world?

Draw Out

A good answer identifies “that the world through Him might be saved” — the purpose is rescue, not judgement. Push gently: “What does ‘the world’ include? Who does it leave out?” New believers will often be surprised that the verse says God did not send Jesus to condemn; probe that surprise. Mature believers should be asked: “Does your daily experience of faith reflect a God who came to rescue, or do you relate to Him more through the condemnation lens?”

Read John 17:3. According to Jesus, what is eternal life? How does this compare to how you would have defined it before this lesson?

Draw Out

The key is “knowing” God and Jesus Christ. Good answers will note that Jesus defines eternal life as relational, not geographical (heaven) or temporal (living forever). Ask: “What changes about how you pursue God this week if eternal life is about knowing Him rather than going somewhere?” New believers: focus on the present tense — “you can have this now.” Mature believers: “How much of your faith practice is actually designed to know God more deeply, rather than to stay in good standing?”

Read 1 John 5:11–12. The verse uses present tense: “He who has the Son has life.” What does it mean to you that eternal life is something you possess now, not only something waiting in the future?

Draw Out

Watch for honest uncertainty here. Many believers have never thought about eternal life as a present possession. Allow people to sit with the idea before jumping to application. If silence follows the question, try: “Is the word ‘possess’ surprising? Why?” New believers will often say they do not feel like they have it yet; that is a perfect opening to explain that feelings follow understanding, not the other way around.

Pastoral Note

Someone in the group may have recently lost someone and be wrestling with questions about the afterlife. Do not avoid the future dimension of eternal life; the lesson holds both present and future. If a grief-related question surfaces, acknowledge the future hope is real, and note that the lesson is widening that picture rather than replacing it.

Read John 10:10. Jesus contrasts the thief with Himself. What does this tell you about God’s intentions toward you?

Draw Out

The contrast is between theft/death/destruction and abundance of life. Good answers will name the two agents and their purposes. The key facilitation move here is to personalise it: “If you believed that everything good in your life came from Jesus and everything destructive did not, how would that change how you respond to what happens around you?” This question bridges into Lesson 13, so file it for later if it generates more discussion than you have time for now.

Pastoral Note

If someone in the group has only ever understood faith through fear of hell, this lesson may be the first time they have heard that God’s purpose in sending Jesus was rescue rather than threat. That can surface a range of emotions including relief, confusion or even mild grief at years spent in a fear-based framework. Allow space for that. You are not responsible for processing their whole theological history in one session; simply affirm what the text says and note that the rest of the series builds on it.

Watch For

Reducing eternal life back to the destination. After the discussion, students may intellectually accept John 17:3 but then reframe their application in purely future terms: “So I will know God fully in heaven.” The lesson is making a present-tense claim. If this reframe appears, draw the group back to 1 John 5:12: the verb is present, not future.

Spiritualising abundance. John 10:10 may prompt someone to claim that “abundant life” means material prosperity. The lesson does not make that connection, and you should not either. The lesson’s context is life in relationship with God versus life under the thief’s influence. Keep the discussion in that frame.

Lesson 2 of 16 | Section A: The Gospel Foundation

Salvation by Grace

At a Glance
Lesson AimStudents understand that salvation is entirely God’s gift received through humility and faith, not earned through religious effort or moral performance.
Key ScriptureEphesians 2:8–9 (NKJV)
Core TruthSalvation cannot be earned by religious effort or moral performance. It is God’s free gift, received by those who humble themselves and call on His mercy.
Sensitivity LevelMedium — The parable targets religious self-sufficiency directly. Anyone with a long religious background may feel implicitly challenged.
Suggested Session Time60 minutes standard.
Core Truth

Salvation cannot be earned by religious effort or moral performance. It is God’s free gift, received by those who humble themselves and call on His mercy.

Opening Question Facilitation

Note

Opening question: “Have you ever felt you needed to earn God’s approval through something you did? Where did that idea come from?”

This question surfaces the performance dynamic directly. Expect a spectrum: new believers who have never thought about it consciously, and mature believers who can name the pattern precisely but may still live by it. Both sets of answers are useful. If someone says “I have never felt that way,” probe gently: “What happens in your mind when you have gone a week without reading your Bible?” That question usually surfaces the underlying earn-it logic. Bridge to the teaching by noting that the parable Jesus tells in Luke 18 is aimed directly at people who have a strong religious performance record — not at obvious sinners.

Core Teaching Walkthrough

Two men, one temple, two very different outcomes. The lesson opens Luke 18:9–14 carefully. The note box flags that Pharisees were not casual religious people — they were the most serious practitioners of their day. This reframes the parable immediately. Jesus chose the hardest possible test case. Emphasise this for mature believers who might too quickly identify with the tax collector and miss that the parable challenges exactly the kind of committed religious person they may be.

The Pharisee’s problem was not his behaviour. The lesson distinguishes between the Pharisee’s actions (fasting, tithing, law-keeping — none of which are wrong) and his orientation (using those actions as a basis for standing before God). The caution box names this clearly: self-righteousness is not always loud or obvious. This is a moment to slow down for mature believers. Ask: “Is there a version of the Pharisee’s prayer that you have said quietly in your head?” Do not rush the room past this discomfort.

What the tax collector understood. The depth box unpacks hilaskomai — the Greek word for “merciful” in the tax collector’s prayer — as propitiation: an appeal to a God who satisfies His own just requirements. The tax collector was not just being polite. He was understanding something profound about how God deals with sin. You do not need to use the Greek term in the group; but understand the point — the tax collector’s prayer was theologically precise, not merely emotional.

Grace: what the word actually means. Ephesians 2:8–9 defines the mechanism: faith, not works. The note box following Ephesians 2:9 is important — it points immediately to Ephesians 2:10, which says believers were created for good works. Good works appear after salvation in their proper place, not as the means of it. This is critical for the group to understand: grace does not remove good works from the picture; it relocates them. They follow from identity rather than producing it.

Grace is available to whoever calls. Romans 10:13 closes the teaching: “whoever calls.” This is the application point. The lesson is offering the tax collector’s posture as the available entry point for anyone in the room, regardless of their history.

Discussion Guide

Read Luke 18:9. Jesus directed this parable at a specific group. Who were they and what was their attitude toward others?

Draw Out

The group trusted in themselves and despised others. Good answers will name both elements. Push on “despised”: “What does it look like when someone’s religious confidence tips into contempt for those who are less religious?” New believers may not recognise this from their own experience; mature believers almost certainly will. The lesson is not condemning seriousness about faith; it is identifying what happens when seriousness becomes the basis for standing rather than the fruit of it.

Read Luke 18:11–12. List the things the Pharisee pointed to in his prayer. What was he actually claiming?

Draw Out

He listed: fasting twice a week, tithing all he possessed, not being like extortioners, unjust or adulterers, not being like the tax collector. He was claiming that his track record entitled him to God’s approval. Good answers will notice that the Pharisee was praying about himself, not to God. Ask: “When you pray, whose record are you presenting — yours or Christ’s?”

Read Luke 18:13–14. What did the tax collector do and say? Why did Jesus say he went home justified rather than the Pharisee?

Draw Out

He would not lift his eyes; he beat his breast; he said “God, be merciful to me, a sinner.” He went home justified because he came with nothing except an appeal to God’s mercy. The tip box from the student lesson is worth mentioning: “notice which person in the parable you imitate when you have failed.” This bridges beautifully to the practical application.

Pastoral Note

Someone in the group may have a significant religious history — perhaps decades in a tradition that emphasised works and performance. This discussion may produce grief or anger rather than simple assent. If that happens, acknowledge it: “It is a significant thing to realise that what you were taught does not match what Jesus actually said here.” Do not dismiss the tradition; do not soften what the lesson teaches.

Read Ephesians 2:8–9. Paul says salvation is “not of yourselves” and “not of works.” What do you think that means for someone who feels their past disqualifies them?

Draw Out

If salvation requires nothing from us to initiate it, then nothing in our past can prevent it from being available now. Good answers will hold both truths: not of yourselves means no achievement qualifies you; not of works means no failure disqualifies you. New believers who carry guilt will hear this as relief; mature believers may hear it as the theological ground for something they have assented to but not fully received. Ask: “Do you live as though your past disqualifies you, even while believing these verses?”

Watch For

Antinomianism: “if works don’t matter, nothing matters.” Ephesians 2:9 without Ephesians 2:10 is incomplete. The lesson addresses this in the note box. If someone moves toward “so I can do whatever I want,” the answer is in the same passage: you were created for good works. They belong after identity, not as the basis for it. The same concern will recur in Lesson 11.

The Pharisee’s log remaining in mature believers. Long-standing believers are at particular risk of identifying too quickly with the tax collector in this parable. The lesson’s caution box names self-righteousness as something that can operate quietly through church attendance and moral record. Do not let the group leave feeling they have defeated the Pharisee’s error without examining whether it operates in subtler forms in their own lives.

Lesson 3 of 16 | Section A: The Gospel Foundation

Righteousness by Grace

At a Glance
Lesson AimStudents understand that righteousness before God is not achieved by personal improvement but received as a gift through faith in Christ, and can identify the difference between the two.
Key Scripture2 Corinthians 5:21 (NKJV)
Core TruthGod does not ask you to improve your own righteousness until it reaches His standard. He gives you His own righteousness as a gift, received through faith in Jesus Christ.
Sensitivity LevelMedium — The imputed righteousness doctrine challenges performance-based Christianity at its root. Significant for anyone whose faith has been primarily about moral improvement.
Suggested Session Time60 minutes standard.
Core Truth

God does not ask you to improve your own righteousness until it reaches His standard. He gives you His own righteousness as a gift, received through faith in Jesus Christ.

Opening Question Facilitation

Note

Opening question: “When you think about standing before God, do you feel you measure up? What do you base that feeling on?”

Most people in the room will answer this question by reference to their behaviour: how well they have been doing, how recently they have failed, how much time they have spent in prayer or Bible reading. Some may say “no, I never feel I measure up.” Both answers are precisely what the lesson addresses. Bridge by pointing to the lesson’s opening move: “The question this lesson is asking is not whether you measure up, but what standard is being applied — yours, or Christ’s?”

Core Teaching Walkthrough

The standard the problem requires. Romans 3:23 establishes that “all have sinned.” The note box flags that “all” is without exception — not “most” or “the obviously bad people.” The lesson is careful to include the religious observer alongside the obvious transgressor. For mature believers who have kept a strong moral record, this is the theological ground-clearing that matters most. “All have sinned” means the standard is not relative; there is no favourable comparison available.

Two kinds of righteousness. Philippians 3:9 is the pivot: righteousness that is from the law versus righteousness that is through faith in Christ. The depth box here unpacks dikaiosyne and dikaioo — both the noun and the verb from the same Greek root, meaning right standing or being declared as meeting the required standard. You do not need to use the Greek; but understand the point: righteousness is a legal declaration about standing, not a description of moral performance.

The great exchange. 2 Corinthians 5:21 is the key scripture: Christ was made sin; we were made righteousness. The note box accompanying it asks students to “read it again and note what moves in each direction.” This is the exchange to emphasise. The caution box addresses a common misreading — that Christ merely covered over sin without dealing with it. Paul’s language is more radical: Christ absorbed the full legal consequence of sin, and the believer receives the full legal status of righteousness. Both sides of the exchange are complete.

Righteousness received, not achieved. Romans 4:3 shows the pattern with Abraham: credited as righteousness, not earned. The note box explains imputation as an accounting term — genuinely credited to the believer’s account, not merely notional. Romans 4:16 makes the principle explicit: “of faith so that it might be according to grace.” The tip box here is a good practical test for the group: “If I fell badly next week, would I feel my standing before God had changed?” If the answer is yes, the confidence is resting on behaviour, not on Christ.

What happens when we try to earn it instead. Romans 10:3 names the failure mode: seeking to establish your own righteousness, being ignorant of God’s righteousness. This is not just an Old Testament problem. The lesson applies it to present experience. Ask the group: “What does it look like when someone seeks to establish their own righteousness today?”

Discussion Guide

Read Romans 3:23. Paul says “all have sinned.” What does that include, and what does it rule out as a basis for comparing yourself favourably with others before God?

Draw Out

“All” rules out all favourable comparisons. No moral record, no religious background, no good behaviour provides a relative advantage before God. New believers will find this levelling and accessible; mature believers may find it uncomfortable because it dismantles the subtle ranking that religious experience can produce. Ask: “Is there anyone you compare yourself to in order to feel more confident before God? What does this verse do to that comparison?”

Read Philippians 3:9. Paul describes two kinds of righteousness. What is the difference between them, and which one does he want?

Draw Out

His own righteousness from the law versus the righteousness which is through faith in Christ. He wants the one through faith. The key insight is that Paul had a very strong personal righteousness record (he lists it in verses 5–6) and counted it as “rubbish.” Ask mature believers specifically: “What would you be willing to count as rubbish in order to have what Paul is describing here?”

Read 2 Corinthians 5:21. Describe the exchange this verse describes. What did Christ take? What did we receive?

Draw Out

Christ took sin; we received righteousness. Good answers will name both directions precisely. Push on the completeness of each side: “The verse says He was made sin — not that He bore sin, but that He was made it. What does that tell you about how completely He took our side of the exchange?” Then: “If you receive His righteousness in that same complete way, how righteous are you?” New believers often find the second question shocking; allow that response.

Read Romans 4:3. Righteousness was “accounted” to Abraham. What did Abraham do to receive it, and what does that tell you about how righteousness works?

Draw Out

He believed. That is all. The accounting language matters: a credited amount is real, not fictitious. Abraham did not perform for it; he received it as a response to belief. The lesson’s note box is worth quoting here: imputation means genuinely credited to their account, not merely politely overlooked.

Watch For

Passive Christianity: “if God does it all, I do nothing.” The lesson does not teach quietism or spiritual passivity. Romans 5:17 — which appears in the application section — speaks of those who “receive” the gift. Receiving is an active posture. The danger of the righteousness doctrine without the identity doctrine (coming in Lessons 9 and 10) is that it can feel abstract. Flag ahead: “This lesson establishes your standing before God; the identity lessons later will show you how to live from that standing.”

Misapplying “all have sinned” as permanent spiritual disability. Romans 3:23 establishes universal need; it does not establish permanent condemnation. Romans 3:24 immediately follows with justification by grace. If the group gets stuck on the diagnosis without moving to the solution, redirect to the key scripture: 2 Corinthians 5:21 is the cure that Paul sets alongside the diagnosis.

Lesson 4 of 16 | Section B: Knowing God

Relationship with God

At a Glance
Lesson AimStudents identify the lie about God’s character that sits at the root of most wrong ideas about Him, and replace it with the truth of Romans 5:8 — that God demonstrated love before any change on our part.
Key ScriptureRomans 5:8 (NKJV)
Core TruthGod’s heart toward you has always been to draw you close, not keep you at a distance. His love was demonstrated before you changed, and it does not depend on you staying changed.
Sensitivity LevelMedium — Questions about God’s character and the problem of evil will be close to the surface; the session touches on human experience of abandonment and distance. Likely to surface personal pain for some.
Suggested Session Time60 minutes standard.
Core Truth

God’s heart toward you has always been to draw you close, not keep you at a distance. His love was demonstrated before you changed, and it does not depend on you staying changed.

Opening Question Facilitation

Note

Opening question: “What is the first word that comes to mind when you think about God’s attitude toward you personally? Where did that word come from?”

This is a diagnostic question for the whole group. Listen for the underlying source of each person’s picture of God: their family, their church background, their own sense of failure, or genuine scripture. Common answers include “disappointed,” “watching,” “patient,” “loving,” and “angry.” Whatever the answers, do not evaluate or correct them yet. Receive them neutrally and bridge this way: “Every one of those words came from somewhere. This lesson traces where our picture of God comes from — and it starts with the first lie ever told about God’s character.” That pivot into Genesis 3 should generate attention.

Core Teaching Walkthrough

The lie at the root of every wrong idea about God. Genesis 3:1–5 is the teaching’s starting point. Satan’s accusation was not that God did not exist but that God was withholding. “God knows that in the day you eat of it your eyes will be opened.” The lesson identifies this as the first lie about God’s character — that His limits are motivated by self-protection rather than love. This framing is the most important thing to communicate in the first half of the session. Ask: “Can you see how that particular lie — ‘God is withholding something from you’ — maps onto experiences people have today?”

God pursued them even after they sinned. Genesis 3:9 — “Where are you?” — is presented as God moving toward humanity, not away from it. This is a crucial corrective to the instinct that sin causes God to withdraw. The lesson makes the point that Adam and Eve hid; God came looking. For people who have experienced distance from God after sin or failure, this is the central pastoral reframe of the lesson. Do not rush it.

God’s love preceded your willingness. Romans 5:6–8 provides the doctrinal anchor: “while we were still sinners, Christ died for us.” The timing is everything. God acted first — not in response to human improvement but into human failure. For new believers, this may be their first encounter with the idea that God’s love is not conditional on their cooperation. For mature believers, it is an invitation to examine how much of their daily relationship with God is shaped by the assumption that they need to cooperate before He draws near.

God is love: the bedrock of the relationship. 1 John 4:8 — “God is love.” The lesson makes the distinction: not that God acts lovingly, but that love is His nature. This is a metaphysical claim, not a behavioural description. What flows from this is that God cannot act in a fundamentally unloving way without contradicting His own nature. The discussion question about John 8:7–11 applies this concretely to the woman caught in adultery.

Relationship available now, not after improvement. The teaching closes by returning to the core truth. The lesson is explicit: the relationship is available now — not after a person reaches a certain standard, not after sufficient repentance has been demonstrated. This is both the pastoral gift and the theological challenge of the lesson.

Discussion Guide

Read Genesis 3:1–5. What was the core accusation Satan made about God’s character? Put it in your own words.

Draw Out

The core accusation: God is withholding something from you that would benefit you. He is not actually working in your best interest. Good answers will identify the accusation of self-protective motivation on God’s part. Ask: “Where do you hear a version of that lie operating today — in culture, in your own thinking?” This opens up the pastoral depth of the lesson without requiring anyone to be theologically sophisticated.

Read Genesis 3:9–10. After Adam and Eve sinned, who moved toward whom? What does Adam’s response tell you about what he now believed about God?

Draw Out

God moved toward them; they hid. Adam’s response tells us he now believed God’s approach meant danger. This is directly pastoral: many people have the same response to God after they have failed — they hide rather than draw near. Ask: “When you have sinned or failed, what is your instinctive response toward God?” The point is not to shame that instinct but to show where it comes from and what the truth is.

Pastoral Note

Some people in the group may have experienced abandonment by a parent or authority figure, and their picture of God has been formed by that experience. Do not push anyone to name this in public; but be aware that this question can surface deep personal pain. If someone becomes visibly emotional, acknowledge it gently and allow the group to slow down. The lesson material is the pastoral resource; you do not need to move away from it.

Read Romans 5:6–8. At what point in humanity’s condition did Christ die for us? What does the timing tell you about the nature of God’s love?

Draw Out

Christ died while we were “still weak,” “still sinners,” “enemies.” The timing tells us God’s love is not a response to human goodness; it precedes and creates the opportunity for human goodness. New believers: “What does it mean that God did not wait for you to be ready before He acted?” Mature believers: “How much of your daily sense of closeness to God depends on how well you have done recently?”

Read John 8:7–11. How did Jesus respond to the woman caught in adultery? What does this tell you about what God is actually like toward people who have failed?

Draw Out

Jesus did not condemn; He did not approve of the sin; He invited her forward into a different future. Good answers will hold both: no condemnation and “go and sin no more.” The lesson is not teaching that God ignores sin; it is teaching that His response to sinful people is rescue rather than rejection. Ask: “If you were the woman in this story, what would you have expected Jesus to say? What does the difference between your expectation and His actual response tell you about your picture of God?”

Pastoral Note

This lesson is one of the most emotionally loaded in the series. The opening question deliberately invites people to name their picture of God, and that picture often carries the residue of painful relationships, harsh religious instruction or significant personal failure. Go slowly. A person who has carried a picture of an angry or withholding God for decades will not have it fully replaced in one session. The goal is to plant the seed clearly, not to produce immediate resolution.

Watch For

Sentimentalising God’s love. “God is love” can be misread as “God approves of everything I do.” The lesson does not make that claim and neither should you. God’s love is demonstrated toward sinners; it is not indifference to sin. John 8:11 holds the tension: no condemnation and “go and sin no more.” If someone moves toward a version of love that removes moral seriousness, the John 8 passage is your anchor.

Dismissing the opening question answers too quickly. If someone says their first word is “angry” or “disappointed,” do not immediately correct them with a Bible verse. Receive the answer, acknowledge that many people share it, and let the teaching make the case. The person who holds a negative picture of God will not be argued out of it; they will encounter a different picture through the scripture and decide for themselves.

Lesson 5 of 16 | Section B: Knowing God

The Nature of God

At a Glance

Key ScriptureJohn 14:9 (NKJV)

Sensitivity LevelMedium — The apparent contrast between Old and New Testament presentations of God is one of the most common stumbling blocks for both new believers and enquirers.
Suggested Session Time60 minutes standard.
Core Truth

Jesus is the clearest and fullest picture of who God is. If you want to know God’s nature, look at how Jesus treated people. That is not a departure from the Father’s character; it is its most precise expression.

Opening Question Facilitation

Note

Opening question: “When you read the Old Testament, does God seem the same to you as the God you see in Jesus? If there seems to be a difference, where do you think it comes from?”

This is a question that troubles many sincere believers, new and mature alike. Expect honest answers in the range of “they seem completely different,” “I don’t read the Old Testament much,” and “the God of judgment versus the God of love.” Accept all of these without correction. Bridge by naming the tension honestly: “This is a question that has troubled thoughtful readers of scripture for centuries. This lesson does not pretend it is simple; it works through what Jesus Himself says about the relationship between His character and the Father’s.” The fact that you name the difficulty builds trust before the teaching begins.

Core Teaching Walkthrough

Why Scripture appears to show two different Gods. The lesson opens by naming the apparent contradiction rather than glossing it. This is deliberate; any approach that says “there is no problem here” immediately loses the people in the room who have felt the problem. The lesson’s approach is to distinguish between what the law reveals about the human condition versus what it reveals about God’s character. This is the hermeneutical key.

The law’s actual purpose: a mirror, not a ladder. Romans 7:7 and Galatians 3:24 are the exegetical basis. The law reveals sin (mirror) and leads to Christ (tutor/guardian). The lesson uses the depth box here to develop the tutor metaphor: a tutor is not a destination. The law was never intended as the means of earning God’s approval; it was intended to demonstrate the impossibility of that project. Emphasise this for anyone who grew up in a law-keeping religious tradition.

God was still present after the fall. Genesis 4:6–9 shows God engaging with Cain after the fall of his parents. This is the lesson’s evidential move: if sin caused God to withdraw, He would not have been speaking to Cain at all. The fact that He speaks — and with concern and warning — shows that God’s relational initiative survived the fall. This matters particularly for anyone whose experience of sin has produced a felt sense of God’s withdrawal.

Jesus: the precise image of the Father’s nature. John 14:9 is the governing principle the lesson builds toward: “He who has seen Me has seen the Father.” Hebrews 1:3 supports this with “the express image of His person.” The lesson is making a strong claim: Jesus is not a softer version of the Father, nor an alternative to Him. He is the precise image. Therefore, the God who heals, restores, forgives and welcomes in the Gospels is the same God at work in all of scripture.

What this means for relating to God now. The application invites students to read difficult Old Testament passages through the lens of John 14:9 rather than allowing those passages to reshape their picture of God’s character. This does not dissolve hermeneutical complexity, but it gives the facilitator a practical tool: “Hold that passage alongside what you know God’s character to be from the Gospels.”

Discussion Guide

Read Romans 7:7. Paul says “I would not have known sin except through the law.” What does this tell you about the law’s purpose? Was the law meant to be the way to earn God’s acceptance?

Draw Out

The law’s purpose is diagnostic, not remedial. It identifies the problem; it does not solve it. Good answers will note that if the law’s purpose was to reveal sin, it could not simultaneously be the means of overcoming it. Ask: “If the law was a mirror rather than a ladder, what does that tell you about the people who were trying to use it as a ladder?” This surfaces the Pharisee dynamic from Lesson 2 again.

Read John 14:9. Jesus says “He who has seen Me has seen the Father.” In your own words, what is He claiming? What does that mean for how we understand God’s character?

Draw Out

Jesus is claiming that His character, behaviour and responses are a perfect picture of the Father’s character, behaviour and responses. The implication is that every time Jesus healed someone, welcomed an outcast or offered forgiveness, He was displaying who God is. Ask: “If that is true, choose one account from the Gospels where Jesus did something surprising. What does that tell you about the Father?”

Think of one account in the Gospels where Jesus healed or restored someone. Who initiated the encounter? What did Jesus require before He helped them? What does that tell you about God’s nature?

Draw Out

This question is open enough that different people will bring different Gospel passages. That variety is the point. In almost every account, Jesus initiates or responds immediately to request without requiring moral qualification first. The woman with the issue of blood, the ten lepers, Blind Bartimaeus, Zacchaeus — none of them had to clean up first. New believers: invite them to share whichever Gospel story they know. Mature believers: push past the obvious answer to the implication — “If this is who God is, why do we so often feel we need to qualify ourselves before approaching Him?”

Watch For

Marcionite drift. The lesson is careful to say that the Old Testament does not reveal a different God; it reveals the same God seen through a different lens. If someone in the group moves toward “the Old Testament God is the bad one,” that is a significant theological error the lesson is specifically designed to prevent. The corrective is Hebrews 1:1–3: the same God who spoke through prophets has now spoken through His Son, the exact image of His nature.

Dismissing genuine Old Testament difficulties. Some passages in the Old Testament are genuinely difficult, and the lesson’s framework does not dissolve that. Do not overclaim. The lesson gives a principle: read through the lens of John 14:9 and ask what the purpose of a passage is given what we know of God’s character. That is a responsible starting point; it is not a complete hermeneutical answer to every difficult text. If a specific hard passage comes up in discussion, acknowledge the difficulty and apply the principle rather than asserting that there is no problem.

Repentance

At a Glance

Key ScriptureLuke 15:20 (NKJV)
Core TruthRepentance is not about reaching a standard before returning to God. It is simply turning in His direction. God’s response to that turning is not a test; it is a welcome.
Sensitivity LevelMedium — The prodigal son parable may surface feelings of guilt, unworthiness or ongoing estrangement from God for some group members. The father’s response in the parable may be emotionally significant.
Suggested Session Time60 minutes standard.
Core Truth

Repentance is not about reaching a standard before returning to God. It is simply turning in His direction. God’s response to that turning is not a test; it is a welcome.

Opening Question Facilitation

Note

Opening question: “Think of a time you were heading in the wrong direction and had to change course. What made you stop, turn around and go a different way?”

This question works on a practical, non-theological level first — a wrong turn on a journey, a bad decision in a relationship, a professional mistake. That non-threatening entry point makes it easier for people to then apply the same framework to their spiritual life. After two or three responses, make the bridge explicit: “Repentance is the spiritual version of exactly what you just described. This lesson is going to look at what that change of direction actually involves, and how God responds to it.” The word “repentance” carries heavy baggage for many people; approaching it through the change-of-course metaphor first disarms some of that.

Core Teaching Walkthrough

What repentance is not. The lesson opens by clearing the ground: repentance is not penance, not self-punishment, not sufficient sorrow demonstrated to God. The teaching is careful not to dismiss the emotional dimension of repentance — genuine sorrow over sin is appropriate — but to locate it correctly. The emotion is the natural fruit of a change of mind, not the currency that purchases forgiveness.

The prodigal son: repentance from the inside out. Luke 15:17–20 traces the prodigal’s repentance through three movements: he came to himself (change of mind), he arose (decision of will), he went (action). The lesson’s subsection heading “repentance from the inside out” names this trajectory precisely. Emphasise that each stage is distinct. Many people mistake the third stage for the whole thing: they confuse the act of returning with the repentance itself. The repentance was the change of mind; the return was the consequence.

The urgency: what is at stake. 2 Peter 3:9 — “not willing that any should perish but that all should come to repentance.” The lesson uses this to reframe the call to repentance from threat to invitation. The urgency is real, but it flows from God’s desire not to lose anyone. This reframing is pastorally significant for anyone who has experienced the call to repent as primarily a judicial demand.

God’s desire: that none should perish. This subsection develops the patient love of God in 2 Peter 3 alongside the father’s response in Luke 15:20. “He saw him while he was yet a great way off” — the father was watching. The image of God watching for returning people is powerful; use it deliberately. Ask: “What does it mean that the father saw him while he was a long way off? What was the father doing between the son’s departure and his return?”

Three things repentance produces. Acts 26:20 gives Paul’s description: repent, turn to God, do works befitting repentance. The lesson is careful about the order. Works come third. They follow from the turning, not the other way around. If someone tries to produce works first in order to qualify for the turning, they have reversed the sequence and created a performance system again.

Heaven’s response. Luke 15:7 — “joy in heaven over one sinner who repents.” Not relief, not grudging approval, but joy. Close the teaching section here and let the emotional weight of that land before moving to discussion.

Discussion Guide

Read Luke 15:17–20. Trace the prodigal son’s repentance through three stages: the change of mind, the decision of will, and the action taken. What was each stage, in your own words?

Draw Out

Change of mind: “he came to himself” — he stopped agreeing with where he was. Decision of will: “I will arise and go to my father.” Action: he arose and went. New believers may find this three-stage map genuinely new and helpful. Mature believers should be pushed on which stage is hardest: “Which of the three do you find most difficult — changing your mind, deciding, or actually moving?” Often the first stage (genuinely changing your assessment of where you are) is the most resisted.

Read Luke 15:20–24. The son had a prepared speech asking only to be a servant. What did the father do instead? What does this tell you about how God responds to repentance?

Draw Out

The father ran, embraced, kissed, clothed, feasted — restoring full sonship before the speech was even finished. The response was disproportionate to the son’s request. Good answers will notice that the father did not ask where he had been, did not require a period of probation, did not accept the servant proposal. Ask: “What did you expect the father to say? How does the difference between your expectation and his actual response reflect the lesson’s core truth?”

Pastoral Note

For someone who has been away from God for a long period — through deliberate sin, loss of faith or painful experience — the father’s response in this passage may be deeply moving and also difficult to believe personally. Do not rush past the emotional weight of this. If someone says “I don’t think God would respond that way to me,” do not correct them immediately with doctrine. Ask: “What would it take for you to believe that this is how He would respond to you? What has told you otherwise?”

Read 2 Peter 3:9. God is described as “longsuffering” and “not willing that any should perish.” How does that change the way you understand the call to repent? Is it a threat or an invitation?

Draw Out

Good answers will identify the call to repent as motivated by God’s desire not to lose people, not by His desire to punish. The word “longsuffering” is key: God is not reluctant to wait; He is actively patient. Ask: “If God is not willing that any should perish, what does that tell you about how He views a person who has not yet turned to Him?”

Watch For

Repentance as ongoing penance. The lesson specifically addresses the misapplication of repentance as a repeated performance of sorrow that must be adequate before forgiveness is available. This pattern is particularly common in people from traditions that emphasise confession as a repeated cycle. The lesson’s teaching is clear: repentance is a change of direction, not a transaction. The prodigal son did not perform sorrow to his father; he turned and walked home.

The elder brother dynamic in the room. Luke 15 contains a third character the lesson does not primarily focus on: the elder brother, who is resentful at the father’s welcome. In a mixed group, a mature believer who has been faithfully serving for years may have an unconscious elder brother response to the idea that return is unconditionally welcomed. If that surfaces in the discussion, do not dismiss it — the parable does not dismiss it either. The father goes out to the elder brother too.

Commitment

Core Truth

Jesus does not ask for partial allegiance alongside your existing priorities. He asks to be first. That is not a demand made by a tyrant; it is an invitation made by the one who already gave everything for you.

Opening Question Facilitation

Note

Opening question: “Think of the deepest commitment you have made in your life. What made you willing to make it, and what has it cost you?”

Common answers: marriage, parenthood, a career change, caring for a family member. The key is the second part: “what made you willing?” Most people will describe a relationship or a love that made the cost acceptable. Use that in the bridge: “This lesson looks at Jesus deliberately thinning the crowd of followers by raising the cost of following Him. But it finishes with something Paul says in 1 Corinthians that reframes the whole thing: you were already bought.” The surprise is that commitment comes after knowing you were loved, not before.

Core Teaching Walkthrough

Jesus deliberately thinned the crowd. Luke 14:25–26 — Jesus turns to a large crowd and says words that would have made many of them stop following. The lesson’s subsection title is important: Jesus deliberately thinned the crowd. He was not trying to maximise his following; He was after something specific. For new believers who may have encountered a very low-cost version of Christianity, this is a significant reorientation. For mature believers, it is worth asking whether the version of Christianity they were given actually asked for this level of commitment.

Three conversations about the cost. Luke 9:57–62 gives three encounters about following. Each person faces a different obstacle: one has an expectation of comfort (no certain dwelling), one has a family duty (bury my father), one has a social obligation (say goodbye to family). The lesson asks what underlying issue each obstacle represents. These are not abstract historical examples; they map onto real obstacles people in the room are currently facing. Leave time for the group to identify their own version.

Count the cost honestly. Luke 14:28–30 — the builder and the king going to war. The lesson’s reading is careful: Jesus is not warning people away from following Him. He is inviting them to count the cost honestly, because a commitment made without counting the cost will not survive. This is the difference between discouraging and demanding.

The foundation of commitment: you were already bought. 1 Corinthians 6:19–20 is where the teaching turns. The commitment asked for is not self-generated; it is a response to a transaction that has already occurred. “You were bought at a price.” The lesson notes that understanding this changes the nature of the commitment from a demand made by a superior to a response made by someone who has been loved extravagantly. This is the pivot of the whole lesson.

What Jesus is not looking for. John 2:23–25 closes the teaching: Jesus did not commit Himself to those who believed because of signs. He was not looking for a crowd of impressed observers. He was looking for genuine faith. The lesson distinguishes between belief-because-of-miracles and the kind of belief Jesus was looking for, without specifying exactly what that is — leaving the question open for discussion.

Discussion Guide

Read Luke 9:57–62. In each of the three encounters, what was the obstacle that stood between the person and wholehearted following? How would you name the underlying issue in each case?

Draw Out

Encounter 1: comfort and certainty (no place to lay His head). Encounter 2: family obligation and delay (let me first bury my father — possibly meaning “let me wait until my father dies”). Encounter 3: divided loyalty (let me first say goodbye). Good answers will name the underlying issues: desire for security, priority of social duty, competing relational commitments. Ask: “Which of these three do you most recognise in yourself?”

Read 1 Corinthians 6:19–20. Paul says “you are not your own; you were bought at a price.” How does understanding that this has already happened change the nature of the commitment being asked for?

Draw Out

The commitment is not self-generated — it is a response to something already done. The price has already been paid. Good answers will note that commitment to someone who has already given everything for you is a different thing from commitment that must justify itself. Ask: “If someone bought you out of slavery at great personal cost, what would your response be? Is that a different kind of commitment than one you make under your own steam?” New believers will find this reframing genuinely freeing; mature believers should be asked how much of their commitment is experienced as a response to love versus an obligation they maintain.

Pastoral Note

Luke 14:26 uses “hate” language: “If anyone comes to Me and does not hate his father and mother, wife and children, brothers and sisters, yes, and his own life also, he cannot be My disciple.” This language can be alarming if it is not handled. The lesson does not give a detailed exegesis of this verse, but you should be prepared to explain it briefly if it surfaces. The meaning is comparative: in Hebrew idiom, “love X and hate Y” means “love X more than Y.” Jesus is using shock language to make the point that His call to commitment is not one priority among many; it is the governing one. It does not mean Christians should mistreat their families.

Watch For

Commitment as the basis for standing before God. Lessons 2 and 3 established that standing before God is by grace through faith, not by performance. Lesson 7 addresses wholehearted commitment. These two truths must be held together: commitment is the response of a person who has received grace, not a condition for receiving it. If someone asks “does my level of commitment affect whether God accepts me?”, the answer is no — and the lesson does not say otherwise. What commitment does affect is the richness of the life Jesus describes in John 10:10.

Watering down the cost. In a mixed group, there may be social pressure to soften the lesson’s demanding edges. Resist this. Jesus deliberately thinned the crowd here. The lesson is not asking the facilitator to do the same, but it is asking for honesty. The cost is real. The invitation is worth it. Both are true at the same time.

Core Truth

Baptism does not save you; Jesus does. But it is the first public act in which a believer declares that they have died with Christ, been buried with Him and risen to new life. The New Testament expects it to follow faith without delay.

Opening Question Facilitation

Note

Opening question: “What do you already understand about water baptism? What questions, if any, have you always wanted answered about it?”

This is a wide-open diagnostic question, and it will surface significant variety: people who were baptised as infants, people who have never been baptised at all, people who are curious, people who are resistant. All of those starting points are valid for the session. Take careful note of who is in which position; it will shape how you handle the discussion. Bridge to the teaching by naming the range: “We are coming at this from several different places, which makes this a good discussion to have together. Let’s look at what baptism actually is, what it actually does, and how the New Testament treats it.”

Core Teaching Walkthrough

What baptism does not do. The lesson opens with the negative: baptism does not save. Acts 10:43–44 shows the Holy Spirit falling before baptism occurs. This is deliberately the first point because the lesson wants to establish that salvation is not contingent on baptism, so that the discussion of baptism can happen without people feeling that their eternal standing is in question. This is pastorally important before the confidence callout is handled.

What baptism actually is: an expression of faith. 1 Peter 3:21 — “the answer of a good conscience toward God.” Baptism is a declaration: the believer is publicly stating what has already happened inwardly. The lesson uses the word “expression” deliberately. Baptism expresses faith; it does not create it.

What baptism pictures: death, burial and resurrection. Romans 6:3–5 is the central teaching passage. Going under the water pictures death and burial with Christ; coming up pictures resurrection. The lesson asks students to see baptism as a physical enactment of the gospel narrative. The form matters: immersion is the consistent New Testament picture, though the lesson does not condemn other modes.

The requirements: repentance and faith. Acts 2:38 and Acts 22:16 — baptism follows repentance and faith. The lesson notes the urgency: “without delay” is the New Testament pattern. This is where the confidence callout applies. Read it, name both positions and handle it as directed below.

Confidence Callout — Handling Believer’s Baptism vs Infant Baptism

The student lesson contains a confidence callout that states this position explicitly: this lesson presents believer’s baptism — baptism that follows personal repentance and faith — as the New Testament pattern. This is the position of Baptist, Pentecostal, evangelical and most non-denominational traditions, including The Well Shrewsbury.

The alternative position, paedo-baptism (infant baptism), is held in Anglican, Roman Catholic, Presbyterian, Methodist and Reformed traditions. These traditions understand baptism as a covenant sign applied to the children of believers, corresponding to circumcision in the Old Testament. Many devout, Bible-believing Christians hold this position.

Language for the facilitator: “This lesson presents believer’s baptism as the position this church holds and as the position the source material takes. This means baptism follows personal faith and repentance. If you come from a tradition where you were baptised as an infant, this lesson is not dismissing that tradition or claiming it has no theological basis. What it is saying is that the New Testament pattern is a person believing and then being baptised. For some people, that means being baptised as an adult even if they were baptised as a child. That is a personal decision for each person to make. I am not going to make it for you tonight.” Do not push. Do not minimise. Let the teaching stand.

Discussion Guide

Read Romans 6:3–5. What three events in Jesus’s story does baptism picture? What does going under the water represent, and what does coming up represent?

Draw Out

Death, burial and resurrection. Going under represents death and burial with Christ; coming up represents rising to new life. Good answers will connect this to Romans 6:4 specifically: “we also should walk in newness of life.” The symbolic power is significant. Ask: “If baptism is a physical enactment of the gospel, what does it mean that the New Testament treats it with such urgency?” For new believers who have not yet been baptised, this may be the question that leads to a decision. For those already baptised as believers, it is an invitation to reflect on what their baptism meant and means.

Read 1 Peter 3:21. Peter calls baptism “the answer of a good conscience toward God.” In your own words, what do you think is being pledged or declared at the moment of baptism?

Draw Out

A declaration of allegiance to Christ, a statement of what has already happened inwardly, a public commitment. Good answers will identify baptism as declarative rather than transformative — it is not doing the work of salvation; it is witnessing to what has already been done. Ask: “Why do you think that public declaration matters to God? Why not keep it private?”

Pastoral Note

A group member who was baptised as an infant and has strong family or denominational ties to that tradition may feel that their background is being dismissed or disrespected. Handle this with genuine care. Affirm that their tradition has a coherent theological position; affirm the personal faith they hold; and separate the question of infant baptism from the question of their standing before God. Their salvation is not in question. The question is whether they wish to make the public declaration the New Testament describes as a believer’s baptism. That is a personal decision; do not pressure it.

Watch For

Baptismal regeneration creeping in through the back door. The lesson is very clear that baptism does not save. If discussion drifts toward “you need to be baptised to be saved,” redirect to Acts 10:43–44 where the Holy Spirit fell before baptism. Faith saves; baptism declares.

Dismissing baptism as unimportant because it does not save. The lesson holds the tension carefully: baptism does not save, and it matters. The urgency in Acts 2:38 and Acts 22:16 (“why are you waiting?”) is real. If someone concludes “since it doesn’t save me, it doesn’t matter when or whether I do it,” that is not the lesson’s conclusion. The New Testament treats baptism as the expected immediate response to faith.

What “new creation” actually means. 2 Corinthians 5:17 — “old things have passed away; all things have become new.” The lesson takes this seriously as a literal claim about what happened at salvation. It is not describing a moral intention or a new start; it is describing a genuine ontological change at the spirit level. This is the foundation of everything in Section D.

Confidence Callout — Trichotomy vs Dichotomy

The student lesson contains a confidence callout on the spirit/soul/body framework. Trichotomy (three parts) is the framework the lesson uses; it is held by many charismatic and evangelical traditions. Dichotomy holds that “spirit” and “soul” in Scripture refer to the same reality from different angles.

Language for the facilitator: “This lesson uses a three-part model of the person — spirit, soul and body — which is one well-established way of reading the New Testament. What spiritual death was. Ephesians 2:1–3 — “dead in trespasses and sins.” The lesson explains spiritual death as separation from God, not extinction. The spirit existed but was cut off from the life of God. The new birth: what it actually does. Ephesians 2:4–6 uses resurrection language. The new birth is as radical as coming to life from death. The lesson wants to establish that the change at salvation is genuine and complete at the spirit level — not partial, not potential, not conditional on future performance.

The sealed spirit: already complete. Ephesians 1:13–14 — sealed with the Holy Spirit. The lesson makes the point that the spirit of the new believer is as righteous as it will ever be in eternity; it is sealed and complete. The ongoing work of sanctification happens in the soul (mind, will, emotions), not in the spirit.

The gap: why you do not always feel new. This is the critical pastoral subsection. The new birth changed the spirit completely; the soul and body are undergoing a process of renewal. Feelings, habits and thought patterns belong to the soul and body; they change gradually. The gap between the spirit’s new reality and the soul’s current experience is normal and expected. It is not evidence that the new creation is not real.

Discussion Guide

Read 2 Corinthians 5:17. Paul says “old things have passed away; all things have become new.” If someone asked you what specifically became new at your salvation, what would you say?

Draw Out

Good answers will distinguish between the spirit-level change (reconnected to God, born again, made righteous) and the ongoing soul-level process (renewing of the mind, changing of habits). New believers may struggle to name specific changes; affirm that the uncertainty is normal and that the lesson’s framework helps explain it. Mature believers should be pushed beyond the stock answer: “Not just theologically — what actually changed at the deepest level of who you are?”

Read 1 Corinthians 6:11. The three verbs — washed, sanctified, justified — are all past tense. How does it change your daily life if those are already settled facts rather than things you are still working toward?

Draw Out

The past tense is the theological anchor. If these things are already settled at the spirit level, the daily life is not about achieving them but about learning to stand in them and live from them. New believers: “What would it mean to relate to God from a position of ‘already washed and justified’ rather than ‘working toward it’?” Mature believers: “How much of your prayer and devotional life is shaped by the assumption that these things are still being worked toward rather than already completed?”

Pastoral Note

A person who carries significant shame or a history of repeated failure may find the past-tense assertion very difficult to receive. Do not flatten the emotional reality of that. The gap between the truth of 1 Corinthians 6:11 and the felt experience of ongoing struggle is real, and Lesson 9 names it. If someone expresses that they find these things hard to believe about themselves, acknowledge it: the lesson is not claiming the experience is complete, only that the reality is.

Watch For

Confusing spirit-level reality with soul-level experience. The lesson carefully distinguishes these. If a student hears “you are a new creation, all things are new” and then says “but I still struggle with X, so maybe it did not happen to me,” they are reading their soul-level experience back onto their spirit-level reality. Redirect to the framework: the spirit is complete; the soul is being renewed. The struggle is evidence of the soul renewing process, not evidence that the new creation is not real.

Over-claiming experiential change too quickly. Some mature believers may be tempted to tell new believers that the new creation means all struggles should disappear quickly. The lesson is explicit that the process takes time. The “gap” subsection is there for a reason; protect it in the discussion.

Opening Question Facilitation

Note

Opening question: “After you have sinned or failed God in some way, what does your relationship with Him feel like? Do you feel you need to do something to get back to where you were?”

This is one of the most pastorally direct opening questions in the series. Almost everyone will have an honest answer that reveals a felt distance from God after sin, a sense of needing to perform repentance before re-entry, or a cycle of guilt and recovery. Receive these answers carefully. Bridge to the teaching: “Every one of those descriptions is a real experience. This lesson is asking whether the experience is an accurate description of what has actually happened to your standing before God, or whether it is your soul’s response to something that the New Covenant says is already resolved.”

Core Teaching Walkthrough

The question Christians actually ask. The lesson opens by naming the real question: when I sin, am I still saved? Am I still right with God? The lesson takes that question seriously and addresses it directly. Validate the question for the group before moving to the answer.

What holds you in position: the New Covenant basis. The lesson’s answer is that what holds the believer is the New Covenant, not their own performance. Romans 4:6–8 and the emphatically negative Greek construction “will never impute sin” are central. The absolute language of the promise is the pastoral point: it is not “God will not usually impute sin” or “God will not impute sin if you repent quickly enough.” The lesson presents the New Covenant language as absolute.

Perfected forever: what Hebrews 10:14 actually says. The key scripture holds two tenses together: “perfected forever” (completed, absolute) and “being sanctified” (ongoing process). The lesson is careful to hold both. The person is perfected forever at the spirit level; they are being sanctified in the soul. Neither cancels the other. Walk the group through both verbs explicitly.

What sin does and does not do to the believer. The lesson is specific about the distinction: sin damages fellowship (the quality of the felt relationship), but it does not destroy the standing (the objective relationship before God). The analogy of a parent-child relationship is useful here: a child who rebels is still the parent’s child. The quality of the relationship is damaged; the relationship itself is not undone. 1 John 1:8–9 — “if we confess our sins” — applies to the restoration of fellowship, not the restoration of salvation.

Confidence Callout — Eternal Security vs Conditional Security

The student lesson contains a major confidence callout on this question. This is one of the most significant theological divisions among evangelical Christians.

The eternal security position (also called perseverance of the saints or “once saved, always saved”) is presented in this lesson. It holds that a genuine believer’s standing before God is not destroyed by subsequent sin. It is strongly held in Reformed, Calvinist, Baptist and most charismatic traditions.

The conditional security position (Arminian, Wesleyan, Methodist, Pentecostal in some branches) holds that genuine believers can lose their salvation through persistent unrepentant sin or wilful apostasy. Hebrews 6 and 10 and passages in 2 Peter are typically cited by this position.

Language for the facilitator: “This lesson presents one of the most debated questions in the history of Christianity: can a genuine believer lose their salvation? The lesson teaches that they cannot — that what holds us is Christ’s finished work, not our own performance. This is the position this church holds. I want you to know that this is not a fringe view; it is held by the majority of evangelical and Reformed traditions. There are also sincere, Bible-believing Christians who hold a different view — that ongoing wilful sin can lead to a loss of salvation. Both positions are arguing from scripture. Tonight’s teaching presents the eternal security position clearly. If your background holds a different view, I am not asking you to abandon it tonight; I am asking you to engage seriously with what these texts say.” Then move on to the discussion questions without opening a debate about which position is correct.

Discussion Guide

Read Hebrews 10:14. Two tenses appear: “perfected forever” and “being sanctified.” What is the difference between these two realities, and why is it important that both are present in the same sentence?

Draw Out

“Perfected forever” is a completed, absolute state at the spirit level; “being sanctified” is an ongoing process at the soul level. Both are present and both are real. Good answers will notice that the perfected state does not cancel the ongoing process, and the ongoing process does not qualify the perfected state. Ask: “If both are true at the same time, what does that do to the question of whether your standing changes every time you sin?”

Read Romans 6:1–2. Paul anticipates the misuse of the grace teaching immediately. In your own words, why does the security of the believer’s standing before God not lead to carelessness about sin?

Draw Out

Paul says we died to sin — meaning that sin no longer has the governing power over us it once had. The security of our standing is not a licence; it is a foundation. Because we are no longer under sin’s dominion, we are free to choose against it rather than compelled to choose for it. Good answers will identify the logic: “shall we sin that grace may abound? Certainly not! How shall we who died to sin live any longer in it?” Ask: “Why do you think people assume that security leads to licence? What picture of God does that assumption reveal?”

Pastoral Note

Someone in the group may be carrying significant guilt or shame over a specific sin, past or present. The opening question may surface this. The lesson is the pastoral resource: their standing before God is held by Christ’s finished work, not by the absence of that sin. Do not rush past personal pain toward doctrine. Allow someone who is genuinely struggling with guilt to hear the teaching at the pace they need. The lesson’s core truth is exactly what they need to hear; bring them toward it gently rather than asserting it over their experience.

Watch For

Antinomianism again. Romans 6:1–2 is in the lesson for exactly this reason. If eternal security is heard as permission to sin freely, Paul’s pre-emptive response is right there. Sin damages fellowship and has real-world consequences even if it does not remove standing. Both realities are in the lesson.

The debate opening up rather than closing down. If you have group members from different traditions on this question, the confidence callout is your tool for managing the moment. Name both positions clearly, state which one the lesson presents and the church holds, and do not adjudicate. If someone pushes for a debate, acknowledge the legitimacy of the question and note that it has occupied serious Bible scholars for centuries; tonight’s session is not going to resolve it, but it is going to look carefully at what these specific texts say.

Core Teaching Walkthrough

The default assumption most people carry. The lesson opens by naming the assumption: if God is sovereign, then everything that happens is His will or at least His permission. This assumption is common across a range of Christian traditions and is often the first response to difficulty: “God must be teaching me something through this.” The lesson does not dismiss the idea that God works in all things (Romans 8:28), but it challenges the assumption that God therefore originated all things.

James 1: God does not tempt. James 1:13–17 — God cannot be tempted by evil and does not tempt anyone. Every good and perfect gift comes from Him. The lesson establishes a clear positive/negative: good gifts upward from God; evil, temptation and destructive things are not from Him. The “no variation” language is important: God is consistently good. He does not sometimes send good things and sometimes send evil things.

John 10:10: two sources, two purposes. Two agents, opposite purposes: the thief comes to steal, kill and destroy; Jesus came to give life. The lesson asks students to use this as a diagnostic tool: “Does this thing I am experiencing match the thief’s description or Jesus’s description?” This is a genuinely practical framework. Note that the lesson does not attribute every difficulty to demonic activity; it establishes that destructive purpose is a clear marker of the thief’s work, not God’s.

Acts 10:38: what Jesus went about doing. Peter describes Jesus as healing “all who were oppressed by the devil.” The lesson makes a specific point: healing was described as deliverance from the devil’s work, not as overruling God’s plan for sickness. If sickness were God’s plan, Jesus would have been undoing the Father’s work. The lesson treats this as a significant point about the theological relationship between God and sickness.

Romans 8:28: what the verse actually says. The lesson is careful here: “all things work together for good” does not say all things come from God. God’s redemptive power works in all circumstances; that is different from saying He originated all circumstances. This distinction is the pastoral gift of this subsection. Someone whose child has died does not need to hear “God planned this.” They may be able to hear “God is at work even in this.”

The practical consequence: submit and resist. James 4:7 — submit to God and resist the devil. The lesson’s application is direct: the distinction between what is from God and what is not determines whether you submit or resist. Submitting to something that should be resisted and resisting something that should be submitted to are both errors with real consequences.

The alternative position — held across a range of Reformed, sovereignty-emphasising traditions — maintains that God ordains or permits all things that occur, including suffering, for His sovereign purposes, and that His glory is displayed through all circumstances.

Language for the facilitator: “This lesson takes a clear position: God does not send sickness, evil or destruction. That is the position this church holds, and it is rooted in texts like James 1:13–17, John 10:10 and Acts 10:38. Both positions are trying to take seriously what the Bible says about God’s power and His goodness. This lesson presents one position clearly so that you can apply it practically. Someone in the group may currently be experiencing significant loss, illness or grief. They may have been told by well-meaning people that God sent it for their growth. Causing something means originating it; redeeming something means taking what has been caused by someone else and working it toward a good outcome. The difference is significant: if God causes everything, then evil, sickness and tragedy are part of His purpose by design. If God redeems everything, then He is at work even in things He did not design. Attributing every difficulty to demonic activity. The lesson teaches that God does not originate evil; it does not teach that every difficulty is directly demonic. Natural consequences, human choices and the broken condition of the world also produce difficulty. The diagnostic is about purpose and character (steal/kill/destroy vs give life), not about ascribing a direct demonic agent to every trial. Keep the group’s application proportionate.

Using Romans 8:28 as a conversation-stopper. The application section of the student lesson explicitly warns against quoting Romans 8:28 to close down someone else’s pain. The lesson is aware of the pastoral misuse of this verse. If someone in the group reaches for it in response to another person’s pain, gently redirect: “Romans 8:28 is a comfort for the person going through something, not a tool for explaining it from the outside.”

Core Truth

The New Testament describes a distinct experience beyond salvation: a baptism in the Holy Spirit that empowers believers for witness and service. Salvation gives you the life of God; this empowerment equips you to live and share that life effectively.

Opening Question Facilitation

Note

Opening question: “When you became a Christian, did someone explain to you that there was more available beyond salvation itself? If so, how did they describe it? If not, does that surprise you?”

Expect a significant range: people from charismatic or Pentecostal backgrounds who have already experienced Spirit baptism; people from cessationist or non-charismatic backgrounds who have never encountered this teaching; new believers for whom this is completely new territory. All three groups are in the same room. Receive all answers without evaluating or privileging any. Bridge: “This lesson is going to look at two distinct accounts of the disciples’ encounters with the Holy Spirit, and ask what the difference between them was. Whatever your background with this topic, the question we are working with tonight is: what does the New Testament actually say about this experience and why?”

Core Teaching Walkthrough

The disciples: two distinct encounters with the Spirit. John 20:21–22 (Jesus breathes on them — “Receive the Holy Spirit”) and Acts 2:1–4 (Pentecost — filled with the Spirit, began to speak). The lesson’s argument is that the same disciples had two encounters: the first at the resurrection (receiving the Spirit as new life) and the second at Pentecost (being baptised in the Spirit for power). The distinction between these two encounters is the exegetical foundation of the lesson’s teaching. Walk through both passages carefully and let the group notice the differences.

The Samaritans: saved but not yet Spirit-baptised. Acts 8:14–17 — Philip preached, the Samaritans believed and were baptised, but they had not received the Holy Spirit. Peter and John came and prayed for them specifically to receive. The lesson treats this as evidence that the two experiences are distinct: salvation had occurred; Spirit baptism had not yet. This is the key New Testament passage for the “subsequence” argument — that Spirit baptism is a subsequent, separate experience.

The purpose: power for witness. Acts 1:8 — “you shall receive power when the Holy Spirit has come upon you; and you shall be witnesses.” The lesson is clear that the stated purpose of this empowerment is witness and service. It is not primarily about personal experience; it is about equipping for mission. This keeps the discussion from becoming purely experiential.

Available and accessible: Luke 11:13. The Father gives the Holy Spirit to those who ask. The lesson closes with the promise of availability. The Spirit baptism is not a reward for spiritual achievement; it is available to every believer on request. This is the pastoral invitation of the lesson.

Confidence Callout — Spirit Baptism: Distinct Experience or At Conversion?

The student lesson contains the most significant confidence callout in Part 1. The question is whether the baptism of the Holy Spirit is a distinct, subsequent experience after salvation or whether it is received at the moment of conversion.

The Pentecostal and charismatic position (presented in this lesson and Lesson 15) holds that Spirit baptism is subsequent and separate: you are born again by the Spirit at conversion; you are baptised in the Spirit as a distinct empowerment for witness. The evidence passages are Acts 2, Acts 8 and Acts 10.

The Reformed and non-charismatic evangelical position holds that all genuine believers receive the Holy Spirit fully at conversion (Romans 8:9 — “if anyone does not have the Spirit of Christ, he is not His”). On this view, what is described in Acts is the normal experience of conversion, not a second experience, or it is historically unique to the apostolic period.

Cessationist traditions (many conservative evangelical and Reformed churches) further hold that tongues and sign gifts ceased with the completion of the New Testament canon.

Language for the facilitator: “This lesson and Lessons 15 and 16 present the Pentecostal and charismatic position on the baptism of the Holy Spirit and speaking in tongues. This is the position The Well Shrewsbury holds, and it is the position of the source material these lessons are built on. This is not a fringe view: it is held by hundreds of millions of Christians worldwide across Pentecostal, charismatic and many independent evangelical traditions. I want you to know that there are also serious, Bible-believing Christians — in Reformed, Anglican, Baptist and other evangelical traditions — who hold that the Spirit is received fully at conversion and that the experiences described in Acts are not a second, separate event. This debate has occupied careful Bible scholars for over a century. Tonight’s lesson presents one position clearly and takes it seriously as the working basis for Lessons 14, 15 and 16. I am not asking you to abandon your tradition. I am asking you to engage honestly with what these texts say and what this experience might mean for you.” Then move into the teaching with confidence.

Discussion Guide

Read John 20:21–22 and then Acts 2:1–4. The same disciples appear in both passages. What do you notice is different between the two encounters with the Holy Spirit?

Draw Out

In John 20, Jesus breathes on them and says “Receive the Holy Spirit” — quiet, individual. In Acts 2, there is wind, fire, a gathered group, and they began to speak in tongues. The contrast in manifestation and circumstance is significant. Ask: “If the disciples had already received the Spirit in John 20, why were they still waiting in Jerusalem for the promise of Acts 1:4?” The question is designed to draw the group into the exegetical logic of the lesson rather than just asserting the conclusion.

Read Acts 1:8. What is the stated purpose of receiving the Holy Spirit’s power? How does that purpose change how you understand the connection between Spirit baptism and everyday life?

Draw Out

The purpose is to be witnesses. Spirit baptism is explicitly missional, not merely experiential. Good answers will note that this relocates the question from “do I want an experience?” to “am I equipped for the life I have been given?” Ask: “If the power described in Acts 1:8 was given for witness, what does that mean for how you try to live out your faith in ordinary life? Is the connection between this power and witness something you have thought about before?”

Pastoral Note

Someone in the group may have had a negative experience with charismatic expression: manipulation, pressure, embarrassment or spiritual claims they found difficult. Those experiences are real and should not be dismissed. Do not equate someone’s discomfort with this topic as theological rejection. Acknowledge the experiences gently: “Some people have encountered this teaching in contexts that felt pressured or manipulative. That is a real experience, and it is worth naming. What this lesson is looking at is what the text says, not the manner in which the experience has sometimes been presented.” Keep the focus on the text.

Watch For

Implying that someone without Spirit baptism is a second-class believer. The lesson does not teach this, and neither should the group dynamic. The Samaritans were genuine believers before Peter and John arrived. Spirit baptism is an additional empowerment, not a marker of superior standing before God. The discussion should invite people toward the experience without implying that the absence of it means anything negative about their salvation or spiritual maturity.

The experience becoming the focus rather than the purpose. Acts 1:8 is explicit: the purpose is witness. If the discussion becomes primarily about the experiential aspect of Spirit baptism, gently redirect: “What would it mean practically for your witness — in your workplace, your family, your friendships — if you had the empowerment Acts 1:8 describes?”

How to Receive the Holy Spirit

At a Glance

Key ScriptureActs 10:44–46 (NKJV)
Core TruthReceiving the Holy Spirit is not a reward for spiritual achievement. It is a gift from a generous Father, available to every believer who asks in faith. The steps are simple: ask, believe, yield and speak as the Spirit gives utterance.
Sensitivity LevelVery High — The lesson continues the Lesson 14 position and adds the practical how-to. The confidence callout references Lesson 14 explicitly. Tongues as normal initial evidence is introduced here. Handle with the same care as Lesson 14.
Suggested Session Time60–75 minutes. Allow time for personal response if the group moves toward actively seeking.
Core Truth

Receiving the Holy Spirit is not a reward for spiritual achievement. It is a gift from a generous Father, available to every believer who asks in faith. The steps are simple: ask, believe, yield and speak as the Spirit gives utterance.

Opening Question Facilitation

Note

Opening question: “Is there a gap between what you believe God has made available to you and what you actually experience day to day? If so, what do you think accounts for that gap?”

This question opens the gap between theology and experience — between knowing something is available and actually having it. It applies broadly: mature believers may identify areas where they have settled for less than what the Bible describes. New believers may be very honest about not knowing what is available. Both are valid entry points for the lesson. Bridge: “Cornelius in Acts 10 was devout, prayerful and generous — and yet there was still something Peter came to bring him that he did not yet have. This lesson looks at his story and draws a simple four-step framework for receiving what is already promised.”

Core Teaching Walkthrough

Cornelius: everything in order, but missing one thing. Acts 10:1–2 — devout, God-fearing, generous, prayerful. And yet Peter came and the Holy Spirit fell after Peter spoke. The lesson makes the point that moral and religious attainment, however genuine, is not the mechanism for receiving the Spirit. Cornelius was not rewarded with the Spirit for his goodness; he received it as a gift when the opportunity was created by Peter’s preaching and the group’s open reception.

What happened when Cornelius received. Acts 10:44–46 — the Spirit fell while Peter was still speaking; they spoke in tongues and magnified God. The lesson identifies the timing (while Peter spoke, before any formal prayer) as evidence of the generosity of the giving. The confidence callout for this lesson addresses tongues as normal initial evidence; handle it as directed below.

How to receive: four movements. Ask, believe, yield, speak. The lesson presents these as the faith posture that positions a person to receive, not as a formula that produces a mechanical result. Walk through each one deliberately. The “yield” and “speak” movements are the most unfamiliar and the most likely to raise questions. Yield: surrender of conscious control; trust rather than management. Speak: the person does the speaking; the Spirit provides the utterance. Acts 2:4 makes this division clear — “they began to speak” and “the Spirit gave them utterance.”

Worship: the climate in which the Spirit moves. The lesson closes by connecting Spirit reception with an atmosphere of worship. This is not a formula but an observation: the Spirit moves in an environment of genuine openness to God rather than intellectual assessment or self-protection.

Confidence Callout — Tongues as Normal Initial Evidence

The student lesson references the Lesson 14 confidence callout and adds a specific point: it introduces speaking in tongues as the normal New Testament evidence of Spirit baptism. This is a further point of division within charismatic traditions and between charismatic and non-charismatic Christians.

The classical Pentecostal position holds that speaking in tongues is the normative initial physical evidence of the baptism of the Holy Spirit. This position is held by Assemblies of God, Church of God and most Pentecostal denominations globally.

A broader charismatic position holds that tongues is one of the gifts available, often associated with Spirit baptism, but not necessarily the only or required evidence.

Language for the facilitator: “This lesson and Lesson 16 include tongues as part of the normal experience of Spirit baptism, based on the pattern in Acts 2, Acts 10 and Acts 19. This is the position the church holds. Some charismatics and Pentecostals would qualify this — they would say tongues is a gift that often accompanies Spirit baptism but is not required. Both positions agree that tongues is a genuine, available gift. The main thing the lesson wants you to know is that receiving the Spirit is simpler than most people make it: ask, believe, yield, speak. Whatever your previous experience or tradition on tongues specifically, that posture is the right starting place.”

Discussion Guide

Read Acts 2:4. The verse says “they began to speak” and “the Spirit gave them utterance.” Who did the speaking, and who provided the language? What does that division of action tell you about how receiving the Spirit works?

Draw Out

The persons spoke; the Spirit provided the utterance. This division is the key to understanding tongues practically: the person is not passive (waiting for something to happen to them), nor are they manufacturing something on their own (inventing syllables). They speak in an attitude of yielded faith; the Spirit supplies the content. New believers need to hear this because the most common barrier to receiving is “I don’t know what to say.” The answer is: you start speaking and trust the Spirit to fill what you yield.

Read Luke 11:13. The lesson identifies four movements: ask, believe, yield, speak. Which of these four do you find most natural, and which do you find most difficult? Why?

Draw Out

This question is an invitation to personalise the framework. Most people find asking relatively easy; believing may be complicated by doubts about worthiness; yielding is often the point of resistance (it involves surrender of conscious control); speaking feels exposed. New believers: “Which of these four feels like the obstacle for you right now?” Mature believers: “Have you moved through all four? At which point did you stop or get stuck, and what helped you move past it?”

Pastoral Note

This lesson may create an environment in which some group members want to receive the Spirit that night. If that happens, it is a good thing, not a problem. Be prepared to pray with people after the formal session ends. Do not create pressure or a manufactured atmosphere; but do not be surprised or resistant if the room moves toward personal response. The lesson ends with an application section that actively encourages asking specifically and directly this week.

Watch For

Framing the four movements as a formula. Ask, believe, yield and speak describe a faith posture, not a ritual sequence that guarantees a mechanical result. If someone follows all four movements and nothing experiential happens immediately, do not diagnose the problem or suggest they lacked enough faith. Encourage them to continue in the posture and to seek prayer with someone who has received.

Making receiving the Spirit a pressure event. The lesson specifically notes that receiving is not a reward for achievement and is available to every believer. The environment should be one of open invitation, not performance expectation. The Cornelius account is helpful here: the Spirit fell on an informal gathering of people who were simply listening to Peter speak. There was no formal protocol.

Lesson 16 of 16 | Section D: Life in Christ

The Benefits of Speaking in Tongues

At a Glance
Lesson AimStudents can name three specific benefits of praying in tongues from 1 Corinthians 14, understand the distinction between tongues as private prayer and public utterance, and know how Paul orders the use of tongues in the gathered congregation.
Key Scripture1 Corinthians 14:2 (NKJV)
Core TruthSpeaking in tongues is your spirit communicating directly with God, bypassing the limitations of natural understanding. It builds your faith, enables supernatural intercession and keeps you in contact with the wisdom of God that your mind alone cannot reach.
Sensitivity LevelVery High — The cessationism vs continuationism debate is the most contested topic in Part 1. The confidence callout is the most detailed in the series. The session closes the series on the most divisive topic. Handle with care and confidence in equal measure.
Suggested Session Time60–75 minutes. Allow time for questions, personal reflection and prayer at the close of the final session.
Core Truth

Speaking in tongues is your spirit communicating directly with God, bypassing the limitations of natural understanding. It builds your faith, enables supernatural intercession and keeps you in contact with the wisdom of God that your mind alone cannot reach.

Opening Question Facilitation

Note

Opening question: “Have you ever found yourself in prayer without the words to express what you wanted to say, or aware that you needed to pray for something you could not fully articulate? What did you do in that moment?”

This question works on a universal human level: the experience of prayer inadequacy is almost universal. Nearly everyone will have felt the gap between the weight of what they wanted to pray and the words available to them. This approach to the opening question is deliberate: it draws the group into the felt need that tongues addresses before introducing the gift itself. Bridge: “That experience — the weight you want to bring to God that your natural language cannot carry — is exactly what 1 Corinthians 14 is describing when it talks about praying in tongues. This lesson is going to look at three specific things Paul says praying in tongues actually does.”

Core Teaching Walkthrough

What tongues is: direct communication between spirit and God. 1 Corinthians 14:2 — “for he who speaks in a tongue does not speak to men but to God; for no one understands him; however, in the spirit he speaks mysteries.” The lesson establishes that tongues as private prayer is directed upward to God, not outward to other people. This immediately distinguishes it from the public use of tongues in a congregation (which requires interpretation). This distinction is important for helping the group understand why Paul’s later ordering instructions do not mean tongues is discouraged in private use.

Benefit one: building yourself up in faith. 1 Corinthians 14:4 and Jude 20 — “he who speaks in a tongue edifies himself” and “building yourselves up on your most holy faith, praying in the Holy Spirit.” The lesson explains edification in this context as spiritual strengthening at the spirit level — analogous to physical exercise that builds capacity over time. The benefit is not primarily emotional, though emotional relief may accompany it. It is the spirit being built up directly.

Benefit two: supernatural intercession. Romans 8:26–27 — “the Spirit Himself makes intercession for us with groanings which cannot be uttered… according to the will of God.” The lesson connects this to tongues: the Spirit interceding through the believer, at the spirit level, with perfect alignment to God’s will. The benefit here is that the prayer transcends the limitation of what the mind knows about a situation.

Benefit three: worship that is spirit-level. 1 Corinthians 14:15 — “I will pray with the spirit… I will sing with the spirit.” Paul is not opposing spirit and understanding; he is adding spirit to understanding. The worship dimension of tongues is described as direct spirit-to-God communication, unmediated by the limitations of natural language. Ask: “What would it mean to worship from the deepest level of who you are, beyond what your words can express?”

Paul’s own practice and the right ordering. 1 Corinthians 14:18–19 — Paul speaks in tongues more than the whole Corinthian church, yet in the gathered congregation he prioritises speaking with understanding. The lesson’s final point is about right ordering, not restriction: tongues has its place in both private prayer and public worship, but the needs of the gathered congregation determine how it functions in each context.

Confidence Callout — Cessationism vs Continuationism

The student lesson contains the most detailed confidence callout in Part 1. The tongues debate runs along three main lines:

Cessationism holds that tongues, along with other sign gifts, ceased with the apostolic age and the completion of the New Testament canon. Primary text: 1 Corinthians 13:8–10. This position is held in many Reformed, conservative Baptist and MacArthur-influenced evangelical traditions.

Continuationism holds that all spiritual gifts including tongues continue as normative for the church in every generation. This is the position of Pentecostal, charismatic and many independent evangelical traditions worldwide. The lesson presents this position.

Open but cautious is a third position held by many in mainstream evangelical traditions: the gifts have not definitively ceased, but they should be exercised with discernment and are not the mark of spiritual maturity.

Language for the facilitator: “This lesson and the two before it present what is called the continuationist position: that tongues and the other gifts of the Spirit are available to every believer in every generation. This is the position The Well Shrewsbury holds, and it is the position of the majority of Pentecostal and charismatic Christians worldwide. I want you to know that there are also serious, Bible-believing Christians who hold that these gifts ceased with the apostolic era and that what is called tongues today is a different phenomenon. The cessationist position has been held by thoughtful scholars for centuries. The continuationist position has been held by equally thoughtful scholars. Tonight’s lesson presents one position clearly and asks you to engage with what Paul actually writes in 1 Corinthians 14. Whatever your previous position, read these texts carefully and decide for yourself what they say. My job tonight is not to convince you that tongues is real; it is to help you understand what the lesson is saying and why, and to give you the space to engage with it honestly.” Then proceed with confidence.

Discussion Guide

Read 1 Corinthians 14:4 and Jude 20. Two separate passages describe tongues as building up the one who prays. What does it mean to be built up in your faith, and why do you think this particular kind of prayer does that?

Draw Out

Edification means strengthening, building up for capacity. Good answers will connect this to the idea of the spirit being directly engaged in prayer — bypassing the limits of what the mind knows — and therefore drawing on the life of God directly. Ask: “If this is how it works, what would it mean practically to pray in tongues regularly? What would you expect to change over time?” New believers: this question opens personal curiosity about the experience. Mature believers who already pray in tongues: invite them to share practically what this has meant for them — without making that testimony a standard others feel they must match.

Read 1 Corinthians 14:18–19. Paul speaks in tongues more than the entire Corinthian church, yet in the gathered congregation he prioritises speaking with understanding so others can be built up. What does this tell you about the place of tongues in both private and public settings?

Draw Out

Private use is unrestricted (Paul speaks in tongues extensively). Public use is ordered by the needs of the congregation (he prioritises understanding so others can be built up). Good answers will hold both: tongues is valued and practised by Paul; it is also subject to ordering when others are present. Ask: “What does it tell you about Paul’s view of tongues that he practises it so extensively privately? How does that balance the caution he applies in public settings?”

Pastoral Note

Lesson 16 closes Part 1 of the series. However someone has arrived at this final session — whether they have received the Spirit baptism during the series, are still considering it, or remain uncertain about the whole framework — end the series on a note of grace rather than pressure. Affirm what has been covered. Note that Part 2 continues to build on the foundations. Do not close with an altar-call pressure environment. If people want to pray together about receiving the Spirit, create space for that naturally after the formal session ends, without implying that those who do not participate are behind.

Watch For

Tongues becoming a status marker in the group. If some group members speak in tongues and others do not, there is a risk that those who do not feel like second-class believers. The lesson is explicit that Paul’s instruction to the Corinthians addresses this: “do all speak with tongues?” (1 Corinthians 12:30 implies not all do, in the context of public ministry gifts). The pastoral response is to hold both realities: tongues is available to every believer; it is not a measure of spiritual status or favour. The fruit of the Spirit (Galatians 5) is the measure of maturity, not the gift of tongues.

Dismissing the cessationist position with contempt. The confidence callout takes the cessationist position seriously. It has been held by serious Bible scholars and continues to be held by significant sectors of the global church. The lesson is not inviting the group to look down on people who hold that position. Present the lesson’s position with conviction and without apology, but also without dismissing those who disagree as unspiritual or biblically illiterate.
